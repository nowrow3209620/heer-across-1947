STATION 1 · 1766

The story is written

Waris Shah finishes his Heer in a small room beside the mosque at Malka Hans, near Pakpattan. He didn't invent it. Damodar had told the story more than a century earlier.

ਯਾਰਾਂ ਅਸਾਂ ਨੂੰ ਆਣ ਸਵਾਲ ਕੀਤਾ, ਇਸ਼ਕ ਹੀਰ ਦਾ ਨਵਾਂ ਬਣਾਈਏ ਜੀ
ਰਾਂਝੇ ਹੀਰ ਦੇ ਇਸ਼ਕ ਦੀ ਗੱਲ ਸੁੱਤੀ, ਨਵੇਂ ਸਿਰੇ ਤੋਂ ਫੇਰ ਜਗਾਈਏ ਜੀ

Friends came and asked me: let us make Heer's love new.
The tale of Ranjha and Heer has fallen asleep; let us wake it again from the start.

Waris Shah, Heer. Editions differ slightly in wording.

The poem opens by admitting it's a retelling. Anne Murphy reads the Hir the same way: a Punjabi classic built from borrowing across Persian, Sufi and wider South Asian traditions.

WHAT I NOTICErefusal · world-making · afterlife

Waris Shah refuses too, in his own way. Damodar's lovers get a happier ending. He gives them a tragic one, where love loses to the family, the clergy and the village.

So the afterlife starts right here. Whoever wakes the story up gets to decide what it says.

STATION 2 · THE QAZI

Heer says no

Heer's family want her married to Saida Khera of Rangpur. She says no, so they send for the Qazi. He argues religious law. She argues back in the same language: only God lasts, and her bond with Ranjha is already known to earth and heaven. In folk memory she even beats him.

ਤੂੰ ਕਿਉਂ ਜ਼ੋਰੀ ਕਰਨਾ ਏਂ ਕਾਜ਼ੀ, ਡਰੇਂ ਖ਼ੁਦਾ ਤੂੰ ਨਾਹੀਂ

Why do you force me, Qazi? Have you no fear of God?

Heer to the Qazi in Damodar's Heer (early 1600s), the oldest Punjabi telling. Her "no" was there from the very first version.

Manzur Ejaz sees her up against everyone at once: her parents, her uncle Kaido, the Qazi, the Kheras, and finally the courts.

MY TAKErefusal · world-making · afterlife

My favourite scene in the qissa. She doesn't run from his religion. She turns it on him, and he loses the high ground.

For a few stanzas she talks as if a woman's own promise counts. That world doesn't exist in Jhang. She argues anyway.

People call her a tragic victim. I don't agree. She loses in the village and wins in the poem, which is why nobody remembers the wedding.

CARRY FORWARDIs a refusal still a refusal if it loses?

STATION 3 · AFTER THE WEDDING

Refusing, even after

The wedding happens anyway. In the Kheras' house Heer refuses with whatever she has left: no jewellery, no bright clothes, no food, no sleep. The marriage is never made real.

Ranjha has his ears split at Tilla Jogian and turns up in Rangpur as a jogi. Najm Hosain Syed traces this figure, the faqir who walks out of the social order, all through Punjabi poetry.

WHAT I NOTICErefusal · world-making · afterlife

Two refusals, two shapes. Ranjha walks out. Heer can't.

The jogi's road was open to men, so he gets to build another life while she refuses from inside a house she was locked into.

STATION 4 · 1947

ਅੱਜ ਆਖਾਂ ਵਾਰਸ ਸ਼ਾਹ ਨੂੰ
Today I call upon Waris Shah

Amrita Pritam, 28 and pregnant, has fled Lahore. On a night train in 1947 she writes to Waris Shah: rise from your grave and turn a new page in the book of love. Once one daughter of Punjab wept and you wrote her story. Now a million are weeping.

This is where the line crosses the border. Sara Kazmi shows Pritam using Heer against the patriarchies of nation and community together.

MY TAKErefusal · world-making · afterlife

This station changed the story for me. I'd always filed Heer under romance. Pritam makes her a language for grief.

What stays with me is who she wrote to. Some readers attacked her for choosing Waris Shah over Guru Nanak. To me that choice is the refusal: a Sikh-born woman fleeing Lahore calls on a Muslim Sufi poet buried across the new border.

Still, something gets lost. Heer's own "no" dissolves into a million daughters. Generous and flattening, both at once.

STATION 5 · THIRTY YEARS ON

Nasreen Anjum Bhatti

Nasreen Anjum Bhatti (1943–2016) was a poet, painter and Radio Pakistan broadcaster who wrote in Punjabi against military rule. She reaches for Heer too, this time against the state and the men who claim to speak for Punjab.

Kazmi reads her beside Pritam. Her last collection, ਸ਼ਾਮਲਾਤ (common land), came out after her death.

WHAT I NOTICErefusal · world-making · afterlife

Pritam asked Waris Shah to wake up. Bhatti seems to talk to Heer directly. Thirty years on, the other side of the border, same story, different Qazi.

Then there's that title. After a border that split Punjab like property, a book called ਸ਼ਾਮਲਾਤ, common land, reads like a small act of world-making.

STATION 6 · SONGS AND REELS

Heer on repeat

Coke Studio Pakistan's season 12 released "Heeray" in its fifth episode in 2019, sung by Rahat Fateh Ali Khan and Aima Baig. Mekaal Hasan Band turned Pritam's poem into a song called "Waris Shah". Listen to both, then scroll a Heer hashtag.

MY TAKErefusal · world-making · afterlife

Honestly? I like Heeray. I've played it more than once.

But after reading the qissa I could hear what's missing: the people she was fighting. Coke Studio hoped the song's message of love would reach "the young Heers and Ranjhas of today". Love. Not defiance.

The hashtag is mostly couples and slow-motion dupattas. Fine. Afterlives change, and Mekaal Hasan Band at least keeps the grief audible. My view, though, is simple: a Heer with no Qazi has stopped refusing.

LAST STOP

Pulling the chain

Every Indian train has an emergency chain. Pull it and everything stops, because something matters more than the timetable. A small refusal of its own. So this is where I stop and look back down the line.

WHAT I CARRIED HERE

  • If every retelling changes Heer, who gets to decide what she means?
  • Is a refusal still a refusal if it loses?
  • Who gets to leave, and who has to refuse from inside the house?
  • When Heer becomes a symbol for everyone, does she stop being herself?
  • Can a story belong to two countries that went to war over a line?
  • When we sing Heer today, who is our Qazi?

Put side by side, they feel like one question. One woman says no to a marriage. Waris Shah makes it tragic. Pritam turns it into grief for a divided Punjab, and Bhatti points it at a dictatorship. Today it is mostly a love song.

Here's where I've landed. Heer is alive only when someone pushes against something through her. As a word for romance she's remembered, but she isn't refusing. The songs aren't wrong. Keeping the refusal alive is the listener's job.

YOUR TURNSo when you next hear Heer in a song, whose refusal can you still hear in it, and what has been softened away?
